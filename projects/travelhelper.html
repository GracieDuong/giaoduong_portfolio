Understanding the Difference: Solution vs. Business Goal

Many product professionals start with: "Let's build a chatbot." That's a solution goal—it describes what to build, not why.

The real work begins with a business goal: "Reduce booking abandonment from 33% to 20%, and increase booking completion rate from 67% to 80%." This explains the impact.

❌ Solution Thinking

  • Build AI chatbot
  • Add payment processing
  • Create booking workflow
  • Design conversational UI
  • Success = features shipped

✅ Business Thinking

  • Reduce decision fatigue via AI
  • Increase completion rate 67% → 80%
  • Lower support load 100 → 60 tickets/day
  • Improve CSAT 3.8 → 4.5
  • Success = metrics improved

Background: The Problem

Travel booking platforms overwhelm users with too many filters, tabs, and choices. This leads to 20–30 minutes of comparison time, fragmented planning across multiple tabs, and frustration when systems cannot handle exceptions or unclear requests.

The research here is behavioral: understanding the friction in a user's decision-making journey, not just listing pain points.

Analysis: Problem → Impact → Solution

I broke down each user problem into how it affects the experience, and what product response I designed to solve it:

User / Business Problem Impact on Experience Product / Service Response
Overwhelmed by too many travel options Decision fatigue and slower bookings Guided conversation: destination, budget, purpose (AI filters options)
Unclear or incomplete user inputs User frustration and drop-off risk Validation + re-prompt flow (no silent failure)
Payment failures during checkout Booking abandonment Retry logic + session preservation + alternative method
No personalized recommendations Lower engagement and conversion AI-driven filtering via conversation (not search bars)
AI can't handle complex requests User frustration, abandonment Human escalation workflow without dead-end

Prioritization (MoSCoW)

Clear prioritization ensures we build the right features in the right order. Every feature ladders up to a business goal:

  • MUST HAVE AI Recommendation Engine — Core value prop, solves decision fatigue
  • MUST HAVE Payment Processing — Blocks launch without it (revenue requirement)
  • MUST HAVE Human Escalation — Critical for trust and handling edge cases
  • SHOULD HAVE Booking History — Increases repeat bookings, builds loyalty
  • SHOULD HAVE Recently Viewed — Reduces cognitive load on repeat visits
  • COULD HAVE Travel Insurance Options — Additional revenue stream (V2)
  • COULD HAVE Group Booking — Advanced use case (V2)
  • WON'T HAVE (V1) Social Sharing — Out of scope, not core to booking flow

📌 Rationale: Focus on core booking flow (recommendation → payment → confirmation). History and escalation are critical for trust. Insurance and advanced features come later when core flow is optimized.

Setup: Structure & Workflow Design

First, I mapped the core user problems into five categories: choice overload, unclear inputs, payment failures, lack of personalization, and unsupported requests.

Next, I designed a BPMN workflow with two explicit pools (User + System) to separate what the user does from what the system handles. This forced clarity on validation, retry logic, and escalation paths.

Workflow Design: Validation, Retry, Escalation, Recovery

With the problem-solution mapping, I created a BPMN-based booking workflow that includes:

  • Input validation before generating recommendations
  • Re-prompt loop instead of dead-end failures
  • Retry logic for payment failures
  • Human escalation path when AI cannot help
  • Positive closure with confirmation and email

Product Decisions & Trade-offs

Product thinking means explaining the "why" behind feature choices, not just what we're building. Here are three major decisions:

Decision 1: Conversational AI vs. Traditional Filters

Decision
Use conversational recommendation instead of traditional filter UI
Reason
  • Users often don't know exactly what they want
  • Conversation feels personal (reduces abandonment)
  • AI can surface serendipitous recommendations users wouldn't filter for
  • Reduces decision paralysis (chatbot does filtering)

✅ Pros

  • Higher engagement (feels human)
  • Better personalization (AI learns preferences)
  • Higher completion rate (less friction)
  • Upsell opportunity in conversation

❌ Cons

  • Slower than instant filters (3s vs 0.5s)
  • AI can hallucinate (show wrong hotels)
  • Users might want advanced filters (star rating, exact price)
  • Mobile typing can be slower than clicking
Mitigation
  • Only call AI after collecting enough info
  • 3-second SLA with fallback to filter-based search
  • Show recommendations + "Refine your search" buttons
  • Human escalation if recommendations are obviously wrong

Decision 2: Human Escalation vs. Full Automation

Decision
Implement human escalation path for ~15% of conversations
Reason
  • User trust: "If chatbot fails, human helps" = confidence to use chatbot
  • Edge cases: 15% of travelers have complex needs (groups, medical, etc)
  • Brand quality: Better to have human save the day than have frustrated user
  • Feedback loop: Support learns failure patterns → improves AI

✅ Pros

  • Higher trust and confidence in system
  • Better customer satisfaction
  • Handles edge cases gracefully
  • Generates training data for AI improvement

❌ Cons

  • Requires support team (cost)
  • Slower than full automation
  • Support SLA must be <5 mins (for 15% of traffic)
  • Quality depends on support training
Implementation
  • Escalate if: AI confidence <60%, user explicitly asks, or 2+ failed attempts
  • Preserve context: Support sees full conversation history
  • SLA: 2 mins average response time
  • Feedback: Log all escalations → analyze → retrain AI monthly

Decision 3: Payment Retry vs. Restart Booking

Decision
Allow payment retry without restarting the entire booking flow
Reason
  • Payment failures are common (fraud checks, network, insufficient funds)
  • If user must restart: 70% abandon (lost revenue)
  • Preserve session = user retries = higher completion
  • Competition does this (Booking.com, Expedia) = user expectation

✅ Pros

  • Higher completion rate (67% → 80%)
  • Better UX (no re-entering info)
  • Captures otherwise lost revenue
  • Reduces support load

❌ Cons

  • Complex backend (session state)
  • Hotel availability might change (rare)
  • User might try different card (fraud risk)
Implementation
  • Lock hotel availability for 10 mins
  • Allow 3 payment retry attempts (fraud prevention)
  • Show error reason: "Card declined" vs "Network error"
  • After 3 failures → escalate to support

Roadmap: MVP → V2 → V3

We prioritize ruthlessly, phasing features to hit business goals incrementally:

Validate core concept + 70% completion rate

🎯 Phasing Logic: MVP proves chatbot works + hits 70% completion. V2 focuses on the 15% gap to 80% + reduces support costs (ROI). V3 unlocks new revenue + advanced features (only build if V1-2 succeeds).

Key Takeaways & Product Thinking

1. Business First, Features Second

This is NOT "let's build a chatbot because it's trendy." This IS "reduce booking abandonment from 33% to 20% using conversational AI, which reduces decision fatigue and increases completion rates."

2. Every Feature Ladders Up to a Goal

Payment retry (feature) → preserves session → increases completion 67% → 80% (goal) → generates $2.5M ARR (impact). Without this clear ladder, features become busywork.

3. Personas Drive Prioritization

Business Traveler pain = speed → design for 5-min bookings. Backpacker pain = cost → show value indicators. Family pain = complexity → ask about kids. Same product, different paths.

4. Decisions & Trade-offs Show Maturity

If you document "Why conversational AI instead of filters?" and "Why human escalation instead of full automation?" you're thinking like a product professional, not just a designer.

5. Metrics Tell the Story

Q1 goal: 70% completion (prove concept). Q2 goal: 80% completion (revenue target). Q3 goal: 60 tickets/day (justify team investment). Each metric explains progress and impact.

Conclusion: From Solution to Business Outcome

The TravelHelper case study demonstrates the difference between building features and solving business problems. By starting with business goals, mapping pain points to outcomes, defining success metrics, and making intentional trade-off decisions, I've translated a product idea into a professional, outcome-focused strategy.

These skills—business analysis, product thinking, metrics-driven design, workflow mastery, and decision documentation—are directly applicable to Product Manager, Business Analyst, and Product Strategy roles.

This is what distinguishes product professionals from feature builders: the ability to ask "why?" not just "what?" and to measure success by outcomes, not output.
